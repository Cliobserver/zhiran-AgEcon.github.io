With Zhiyao Ma and Pierre Mérel. China began growing genetically modified (GM) soybeans and maize at scale in 2025 to reduce its dependence on imported crops, causing a productivity shock in a country that buys two thirds of the soybeans traded worldwide. We ask whether Chinese nationwide GM adoption can deliver the self-sufficiency it targets, and who would gain or lose from it globally. In a calibrated model of world crop supply, demand, and Armington trade, a 10% yield gain and the traits' cost savings raise China's soybean self-sufficiency by 1.2 percentage points. Imports supply more than four fifths of Chinese use, and under the Armington assumption, the domestic price, not the import bill, absorbs the shock. The value of the technology lies elsewhere. It raises world welfare by 10.5 billion US dollars a year, nearly all of it accruing to Chinese crop users. Chinese growers of rice and wheat lose, and US farm income falls by 1.0 billion. In contrast, China's 2025 retaliatory tariff on US soybeans, solved on the same baseline, raises self-sufficiency by 0.5 points at no net cost to China and costs US farmers 4.3 billion and the world 0.4 billion a year.
